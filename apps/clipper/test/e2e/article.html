Borrow checking, ten years on

By Rowan Keld, published 14 February 2026

The borrow checker began as a way to make one guarantee cheap to state: that no two parts of a program hold a mutable reference to the same value at the same moment. Ten years of compilers later, the guarantee has not moved, and almost everything around it has.

The first version reasoned about whole lexical scopes, which meant a reference borrowed at the top of a function was borrowed at the bottom of it whether anything read it or not. Programs were written around that, with blocks introduced to end borrows early and temporary bindings introduced to start them late. The shapes those workarounds left behind are still visible in libraries of that era.

Non-lexical lifetimes replaced scopes with the region a reference is actually live in, and a great deal of that ceremony went away in one release. What replaced it was harder to explain rather than harder to satisfy: an error that used to point at a closing brace now points at the last use of a value, which is correct and takes longer to read.

The interesting part of the next ten years is not the checker but the diagnostics. A rule nobody can act on is a rule nobody keeps, and the compiler spends more code on saying what to do about a borrow than on deciding whether the borrow is allowed.
